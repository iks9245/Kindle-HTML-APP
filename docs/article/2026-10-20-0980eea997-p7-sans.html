小中大切換為襯線

Horrible, unmanly, indispensable

The Medici were not the only leading family of Renaissance Italy to take an interest in this new military technology. In 1538, the duke of another Italian city-state, Urbino, described a novel firearm as ‘very dear to me’. Such appreciation, however, ran counter to the dominant image of guns in the period’s literature. Leonhard Fronsperger, once a captain of the ducal armoury in Munich, wrote of gunpowder as ‘indispensable’, but also as ‘horrible’ and ‘unmanly’. Ludovico Ariosto’s epic poem Orlando Furioso (1532) described guns as a ‘foul and pestilent discovery’, weapons through which ‘no more shall gallantry, no more shall valour prove their prowess’. Only in edgier texts, like the swashbuckling memoirs of the goldsmith and sculptor Benvenuto Cellini, did anyone hint that guns were fun. ‘Frightened’ by a plague that hit Rome in the early 1520s causing thousands of deaths, Cellini ‘began to seek relief in a sport that I found very enjoyable’. In between sketching the city’s ancient ruins, he shot pigeons. He developed his own gunpowder recipe, and boasted of his ‘really good marksmanship’, claiming to be able to shoot ‘200 yards point-blank’. This is probably exaggerated, but just as relevant is Cellini’s explanation that, by getting him out into the fresh air to go hunting, and combating his ‘naturally melancholy disposition’, his gun ‘brought me more gain than loss’.
