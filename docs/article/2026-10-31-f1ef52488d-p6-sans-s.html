A New Map of ‘Empty’ Space Challenges a Tranquil Picture of the Galaxy

Gaia observed almost 2 billion stars from different angles as the Earth moved from one side of the sun to the other during its yearly orbit. Studying the effects of parallax allowed researchers to determine the stars’ distances from Earth.

Scientists then studied how the light from those stars dimmed and reddened as it traveled through the ISM on its way to Gaia. By collecting this data from a variety of angles, and by using the distance information they’d gathered through parallax, scientists were able to build up a better understanding of the density of the dust between stars.

But turning that information into a 3D map of the ISM was not straightforward. Many possible configurations of the ISM could satisfy the same data, said Gregory Green, an astronomer at Westlake University in Hangzhou, China. More data offered the astronomers better constraints, but it also complicated their calculations. The challenge, Green said, is picking out the most physically plausible arrangement when the underlying math “becomes impossible.”
