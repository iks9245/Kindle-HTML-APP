小中大切換為無襯線

The self-making world

By daring to usurp God’s monopoly on Creation and reassign it to mortal beings, Lamarck provoked the ire of the mighty and powerful. He wrote that life originated in spontaneous generation, brought about by natural forces such as electricity and heat acting upon inanimate matter. This led to the emergence of the simplest organisms: single-celled protozoa and algae. From that point, a property intrinsic to living matter exerted itself, a tendency to complexify, which Lamarck named ‘the power of life’. As organisms grew more complex, another cause of transformation came into play: their responsiveness to the environment. In the simplest beings, this responsiveness was rudimentary – an irritability or sensitivity, and reflexive reaction. But in birds and mammals, Lamarck argued, it rose to the level of ‘acts of will’, through which creatures could form ‘habits’ and ‘ways of life’ in response to their circumstances. By means of these behaviours, they directed their inner fluids toward the limbs and organs they used most. (Darwin would later propose a similar idea. In 1868, he suggested that parts of the body threw off particles of inheritance, called ‘gemmules’, which were modified by an animal’s behaviours and could carry the modifications into its offspring.)
