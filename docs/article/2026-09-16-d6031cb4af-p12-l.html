Chasing la dolce vita

Between the unification of Italy, in 1861, and 1985, around 29 million Italians left the peninsula. Political instability, high birth rates, natural disasters, unprofitable sharecropping (especially in the South), deprivation and overpopulation were major drivers for this exodus. And the more people left en masse, the more they encouraged others to join them. Today, about 80 million people outside Italy claim full or partial Italian ancestry. This diaspora has generated Little Italys across the world: in New York’s Manhattan, in La Boca, Buenos Aires, and in Clerkenwell, London. Once oases of solidarity, home-away-from-home citadels, where you could find anything – from the Italy football kit to keyrings with Leonardo da Vinci’s Vitruvian Man – they now mostly feel like places of historic, even faded, hyper-Italianness. By 1920, São Paulo alone was home to 70 per cent of the Italians who emigrated to Brazil; as many as 60 per cent of Argentinians have a link to Italy, and Italian is still one of the main mother tongues spoken in Toronto, Canada. In Australia, 4.4 per cent of the population is of Italian heritage. In America, around 17 million people identify as Italian.
