小中大切換為襯線

Chasing la dolce vita

For bona-fide Italians, a tomato sauce is never just a sauce, but a form of alchemy. In an ideal world, it is seasoned with herbs once grown by the Romans, made with San Marzano tomatoes nourished by volcanic soil, and olive oil from Puglia’s ancient groves. This level of culinary precision largely explains why Italians are baffled, outraged even, by foreigners committing offences with their cuisine – snapping dry spaghetti in two before cooking it, grating parmesan onto seafood risotto, or mixing garlic and onion as a base for the same sauce. It might seem precious to outsiders, but a belief in excellence and the purity of traditions underpins Italianness. Indeed, something akin to the formula for tomato sauce can be applied to all areas of Italian production, from coffee machines and scooters to architecture. For my father and grandparents, Italian ingenuity stemmed from the peninsula’s endemic flair. A Lamborghini or a Gio Ponti chair was simply the latest layer of sediment in an accretion of refinement that had already given rise to Brunelleschi’s Dome and Scarlatti’s sonatas.
