They lack the interpretative resources to understand and share significant aspects of their experience

‘No baby meant no loss.’ Many forms of non-death loss are what the family therapist Pauline Boss calls ‘ambiguous loss’. These are losses without clear boundaries, without a definite beginning or end – cases where someone is physically present but psychologically gone, or physically gone but psychologically still there. Think of an Alzheimer’s patient whose self has been erased by the disease or of a soldier missing in action. Because many non-death losses are ambiguous, the grief that follows them is not openly acknowledged, socially validated or publicly mourned – what the grief scholar Kenneth Doka has called ‘disenfranchised grief’.
