How intuitions work

These seemings have an unmistakable epistemic pull. Because your friend’s pain seems like a reason, we accept it as such: the seeming is the reason. What is striking is not simply that we have such seemings, but that we trust them. They are involved in shaping our deliberations, guiding our actions, and eliciting belief. They do not merely occur to us; they appear to reveal something about how things must be in the imagined scenario.

For all their ordinariness, however, intuitions remain puzzling. In perceptual experience, we at least have an established framework: the world impinges on our senses, and different perceptual modalities reinforce one another. In the end, we can trace the causal route it takes to produce them. Intuition offers no such apparatus. Its verdicts simply present themselves, with no organ at work and nothing we can point to that they make contact with. Consequently, we rely on them without a clear account of their nature or epistemic authority. But before we can understand intuitions themselves, we need to situate them within a broader epistemological landscape, one shaped by attempts to unify the justification of our basic beliefs.
