The ladder of knowledge

The orthodox response was to keep the truth and give up the proof. There is a realm of mathematical fact, standing independent of us, and axiomatic systems reach only part of it; incompleteness measures the distance between what is so and what we can show. Its most committed advocate was Gödel himself. However, two problems come with the realm. First, with logical positivism in ruins, Kant’s problem returns: if mathematical truth is independent of the mind, how can it structure sciences that exist within the mind? Second, the realm is posited chiefly to spare us two unwelcome alternatives – psychologism on one side, Brouwer’s restrictions on the other. Avoiding two conclusions is not an argument for a third.

In 1973, the British philosopher Michael Dummett was invited to deliver the annual philosophical lecture at the British Academy. He took as his subject ‘The Justification of Deduction’ – how to justify the ordinary practices of mathematicians without helping oneself to the assumption that their subject matter exists in a realm of its own. The problem, he told his audience, was perhaps ‘one of the most fundamental and intractable problems … in all philosophy’.
