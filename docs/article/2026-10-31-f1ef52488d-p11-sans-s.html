The model could have overestimated the amount of unstable ISM if cold and warm sections were too close together for the model to tell them apart, McKee said. Alternatively, the method the team used to calculate the overall distribution of gas and dust could have led them astray, said Philipp Frank, an astrophysicist at the Kavli Institute for Particle Astrophysics and Cosmology at Stanford. The researchers used an “ensemble-averaged mean,” a calculation that naturally tends to smooth over sharp contrasts. If a cold cloud transitioned abruptly to a warm one, this method would artificially soften its steep drop in density into a more gradual slope, which would inflate the amount of unstable ISM.

Shelest and Bialy — whose analysis is currently undergoing peer review — acknowledge that limited resolution and map smoothing are both issues, but they argue that mathematical blurring alone cannot account for the majority of the unstable ISM. When the authors tested their modeling on an entirely different dust map with lower resolution, the unstable fraction shifted by only about 5%, they said.
