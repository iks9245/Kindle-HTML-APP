Their confidence was misplaced, however. When King Charles VIII of France set out to conquer Naples in 1494, Italy was plunged into a series of devastating wars that would rage, on and off, for much of the following 65 years. Venice could not help being drawn into the fray. Yet if it hoped to profit from the fighting, it was cruelly mistaken. It was woefully unprepared. And as its military position worsened, it found that it had no choice but to call on the Jews for help. Heavy taxes and loans were asked of Jewish communities throughout the terraferma (mainland); and, with food prices rising by the day, Venice did whatever it could to keep Jewish credit flowing. On 14 January 1503, the government offered the Jewish bankers of Mestre a new condotta on unusually generous terms. Provided they continued to lend money at reasonable rates of interest, they would be allowed to come and go as they wanted, to rent houses in Venice, and to observe their rites. Most importantly, they were also given an assurance that, if the war on terraferma threatened their safety at any point, they would be allowed to take refuge in Venice itself, and to remain there as long as necessary.
