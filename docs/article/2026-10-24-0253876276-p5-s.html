Malaria Parasite Caught in the Act of Breaking and Entering

Attempts to image the parasites in the act of breaking and entering got off the ground in the 1970s, when pioneering microbiologists first applied electron microscopy. These scientists observed a distinct thickening of the cellular membrane near the parasite’s access point. Then the tiny, round Plasmodium seemed to simply merge with the cell, like a water droplet coalescing into a larger pool. It would be decades before techniques developed enough to isolate the proteins that make this possible, much less image them.

Another hurdle is that malaria parasites have a very complicated life cycle. They are most at home in the gut of a mosquito where they can reproduce sexually. Humans are what’s known as a reservoir host — a place for the parasite to live while it waits to be taken up by an insect.

“The food supply happens to be our red blood cells,” said Kirk Deitsch, a microbiologist and immunologist at Weill Cornell Medicine in New York. “That’s why they’re invading us.”
