So Shelest, Bialy, and their colleagues set out to combine the 200 gigabytes of data in the two maps. At every point in space, they compared the density of the dust with the intensity of the radiation to understand where the ISM was hot and where it was cool. Eventually, they were able to combine the dust and radiation information on a three-dimensional grid.

Shelest then set out to see what the map could tell him. The biggest surprise came when he investigated the ISM’s overall temperature: Shelest found that 27% of the ISM in our galactic neighborhood was cold, 32% was warm, and the remaining 41% sat squarely in the forbidden zone as unstable medium. “I thought I was wrong,” Shelest said. “How do you go to your science adviser and tell him that you get so much unstable media?”

But the map didn’t appear to be broken. Shelest saw no evidence of artifacts or random statistical noise in the 3D grid. Instead, the unstable ISM formed massive, coherent blankets that wrapped around the cold clouds completely.
