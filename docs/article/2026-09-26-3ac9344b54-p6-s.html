Habermas answered my question by going back further than any of that. He’d been raised Lutheran. Visiting the US in the 1960s and ’70s, he’d been introduced to the Quakers, and something in their small meetings had stayed with him, specifically the way they sought unity with everyone present. When he came to describe the conditions under which people could reason together as equals, he said he heard an echo of those meetings: conditions that hold only when everyone who could be affected by a norm isn’t just included but has a voice, can object, and speaks with sincerity. In those conditions, which go far beyond mere inclusion, the only constraint left on discourse is ‘the unforced force of the better argument’, as he put it in Justification and Application (1993). It didn’t feel like something he’d have said at the podium.
