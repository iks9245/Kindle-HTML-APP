Paternity is poetical

The creation of the father is also the creation of the poet. In the spring of 1797, Coleridge was writing 50 lines of blank verse a day. He began to develop his ‘conversation poems’, which drew from his letters to friends and family: their language is intimate, domestic; their topics often autobiographical. Towards the end of that year, he turned to what are now called his ‘mystery poems’, and are those for which he is most celebrated: ‘Kubla Khan’, ‘Christabel’, ‘The Rime of the Ancient Mariner’, all of which were begun or completed within 18 months of Hartley’s birth.

This was a moment of golden invention, and Coleridge knew that something had changed. Years later, in his rambling autobiography Biographia Literaria (1817), he recalled the transformation. ‘I retired to a cottage in Somersetshire,’ he wrote, and:

Here I found myself all afloat. Doubts rushed in … The fontal truths of natural religion and the books of Revelation alike contributed to the flood; and it was long ere my ark touched on an Ararat, and rested.
