小中大切換為襯線

Where a land ethic blooms

Investing in local food and energy production creates demand for more types of agriculture, renewing ecological and social diversity across landscapes long simplified in the name of industrial efficiency. Such landscapes might include small, intensively cultivated vegetable fields and orchards supplying fresh produce and local processors; trellised hops adapted to local conditions and supporting regional breweries; perennial grasslands producing healthy animal products from cattle, sheep, swine and poultry; small fields of corn, wheat and other grains integrated with livestock or supplying locally owned mills; and oak woodlots thinned to provide firewood and lumber. Wetlands now choked with reed canarygrass and shrubs could once again support wildlife while providing hay, bedding and renewable biomass capable of heating homes, businesses and schools during winter’s deep freeze. By turning more of our dollars away from large agribusiness and toward these small-scale, locally integrated systems, we will be rewarded with healthier soil, cleaner and more abundant groundwater, fewer and less severe floods following intense rains, stronger communities with the skills to manage biologically complex farming systems, and a richer, more secure tapestry of semi-natural-to-wild habitats. Many semi-domestic parts of the rural landscape are important for the wild creatures that enrich our lives and testify to the care we take of the land. The touchstones of these partly wild/partly human landscapes are small pastures and sweeping grasslands, scattered woodlots and deep forests, shady valleys and sandstone-capped bluffs, clean headwater springs, and wetlands alive with waterfowl.
