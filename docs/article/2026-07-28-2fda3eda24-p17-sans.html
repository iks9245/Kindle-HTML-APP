While the study has been a leap forward for rumen microbiology, there are a few caveats. “This paper, important as it is, still has huge gaps connecting it to methane production,” Mackie said. While the head-box experiment is helpful, it doesn’t close the case on how different ciliate species, or the hydrogenobodies themselves, contribute to methane emissions. Importantly, he noted, the authors did not specify the cows’ diet — a primary factor in microbial methane-making, since different feed types result in different amounts of hydrogen gas.

Already, the study has inspired Mackie to explore new research questions. Instead of looking at hydrogen transfer between different species of bacteria and methanogens, “I think that now I should be looking at interkingdom hydrogen transfer,” he said, between bacteria, archaea, fungi, and protozoans.
