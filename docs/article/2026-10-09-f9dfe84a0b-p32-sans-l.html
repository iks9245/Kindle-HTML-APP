The ghetto on the lagoon

The Ghetto was never meant to be anything more than a temporary measure – a quick fix for a passing ‘problem’. But over the decades that followed, the persistence of war and an abiding reluctance to establish a monte di pietà in Venice prevented the government from ridding itself of the city’s Jewish quarter. In fact, as time wore on, the steady decline of Venice’s fortunes not only guaranteed the Ghetto’s endurance – it also paved the way for its expansion. Later, two further ghettos would be created – the Ghetto Vecchio (1541) and the Ghetto Nuovissimo (1633) – each intended to accommodate different groups of Jewish migrants from the Iberian peninsula (those who came via the Ottoman Empire being known euphemistically as ‘Levantines’, and those who subsequently arrived by a more direct route as ‘Ponentines’), whose involvement in international trade increasingly sustained Venice’s faltering commercial standing. And though the Ghetto itself would endure its own decline, it would not be abolished until after the arrival of Napoleon in 1797.
