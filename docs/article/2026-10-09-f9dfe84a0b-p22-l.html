The ghetto on the lagoon

In the Republic of Venice, the friars’ message struck a chord. Since the 1420s, many of Venice’s nearby subject towns on the Italian mainland had grown increasingly hostile to their Jewish residents. Shortly after the promulgation of Dudum ad nostram audientiam, several of them had already banned Jews from lending money, or expelled them outright. And that was just the beginning. When, in 1475, the Jews in Trent (now Trentino) were arrested on suspicion of having ritually murdered a toddler named Simon, a new wave of antisemitic fury was unleashed. Encouraged by preachers like Bernardino da Feltre and Marco da Montegallo, this led to the establishment of monti di pietà in Vicenza, Brescia and Verona just a few years later.
