Sigmund Freud understood this, though he read it as pathology. In the essay ‘Mourning and Melancholia’ (1917), he contended that grief is healthy and normal, but only if it is intelligible and bounded. The job of mourning, he argued, is to detach you from the lost object and reinvest your emotional energy elsewhere. If you can’t complete that job, if the loss is unclear or unnamed or ambiguous, you are no longer mourning. Instead, you are suffering from an illness called melancholia. For Freud, grief that cannot do its work calcifies into something darker, something more elusive.

If you deny someone the right to grieve, you deny them access to themselves

But the damage runs deeper than that. When we cannot grieve a loss, we lose something else along with it: the chance to register what mattered to us.
