Love with nowhere to go

The tradition I spent most of my life in – the lineage of Western thinkers who asked how a human being should live – was the first place I looked to understand what was happening to me. These thinkers had a lot to say about grief, but most of it was suspicious and sceptical. Plato believed a good man is the most self-sufficient of all, and least shaken by loss. In the Phaedo, Socrates’ wife Xanthippe is led away wailing before the philosophy can begin, and towards the end of the dialogue, Socrates rebukes his weeping friends for behaving like women. A similar sentiment can be found among the Stoics. Epictetus, in the Enchiridion, compares the loss of a wife or child to a jug breaking. And Marcus Aurelius, who lost many of his own children, reminds himself in his Meditations not to pray that he might keep his child, but to pray that he might not fear losing him.
