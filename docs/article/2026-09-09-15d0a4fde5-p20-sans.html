Popular attitudes toward immigrants had fluctuated with economic conditions but, for decades, flaring anti-immigrant sentiment had had little effect on general policy. True, the US barred the entry of prostitutes in 1875, convicts and the insane in 1882, and polygamists in 1891, but other than the 1882 blanket ban on Chinese nationals, most legislation had rather narrow targets against individuals defined as undesirables. Otherwise, the doors were mostly open. Policies, however, changed abruptly in the 1920s, and the highway’s promotion had some impact on the reversal. The same year that Coolidge spoke in favour of building the highway, he also declared that ‘America must be kept American,’ and US Congress passed the broadly restrictive Johnson-Reed Act, whose discriminatory quotas reduced immigrant arrivals by a harsh 80 per cent. Latin Americans were not included in the act, but already by 1929 the US began detaining and repatriating individuals of Mexican descent, some of whom had been born in the US.
