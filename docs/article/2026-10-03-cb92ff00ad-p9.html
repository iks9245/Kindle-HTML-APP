In the decades after those pioneering studies reported an inverse flux in two-dimensional systems, physicists did not deeply interrogate whether those flows were fixed. The underlying energy mechanisms of two-dimensional turbulent systems certainly weren’t on the mind of Xinyu Si around 2021, when he started investigating brine shrimp as a student in Fang’s lab.

Swimming in the Energy Flow

Fang’s lab focuses on “active matter,” a physics term describing things that move by themselves and inject energy into their environments. It’s a label broad enough to include networks of living things, like bacteria, and nonliving things, like tiny robots.
