Such pragmatic ambiguity might easily have continued indefinitely, had a new wave of antisemitic hostility not swept Europe in the mid-15th century. In response to a complaint from King John II of Castile about the ‘scandalous’ offences Jews were supposedly committing against Christians in his kingdom, Pope Eugenius IV promulgated a bull – Dudum ad nostram audientiam (1442) – that attempted to separate the two religions as completely as possible. All the privileges Jews had previously enjoyed were annulled; harsh new legal restrictions were imposed; and the opportunities for social interaction were cut down to an absolute minimum. Officially, this was intended to apply only to Castile; but in Italy, the mendicant orders were not slow to pick up on its implications. Wandering from town to town, itinerant friars like Domenico da Leonessa preached against Jewish usury and urged municipal authorities to expel Jewish populations once and for all.
