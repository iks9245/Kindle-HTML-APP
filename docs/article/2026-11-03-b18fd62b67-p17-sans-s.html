And this brings us back to the idea that got me started: muon-catalyzed fusion. In other experiments, when the exotic molecule reaches its final configuration, it is so compact that the two hydrogens fuse almost instantly. This process happens at room temperature, so in that sense it is “cold,” but otherwise it’s very different from the 1989 experiment — not least because it really does happen. The muon is typically ejected, freeing it to create a new exotic atom and restart the cycle; thus, the muon is acting as a catalyst, a nuclear matchmaker. If it could repeat the trick 300 times or more, the process would produce more energy than it consumed. Most physicists doubt it could reach 300, but Okada said he and his team don’t entirely rule it out. Still, the particles released by the fusion reactions could be harvested to synthesize medical isotopes or process nuclear waste from fission reactors. “Even without reaching energy breakeven, muon-catalyzed fusion could be useful,” Okada said.
