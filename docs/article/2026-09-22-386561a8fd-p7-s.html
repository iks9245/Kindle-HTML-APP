He was probably right

Both pathologies make the same mistake: they treat doubt and certainty as the only two options. Neither leaves room for what we actually need: calibrated confidence, the ability to say This is the best available account, I will act on it, and I remain open to being wrong. What is missing, then, is a philosophy of provisional but committed belief, a philosophy Cicero already worked out, in Latin, 2,000 years ago.

It began with a visit to Rome, in 87 BCE, by the then-head of the Platonic Academy, Philo of Larissa, having fled Athens amid political upheaval that eventuated, ironically, in the Roman sack of the city. Cicero, 19 years old and already a prodigy in oratory and law, went to hear Philo and was transfixed. What the visitor taught was not the abstract metaphysics of Plato but something more practical: a method based on the Socratic tradition of enquiry, the radical idea that wisdom begins in recognising the limits of one’s own knowledge.
