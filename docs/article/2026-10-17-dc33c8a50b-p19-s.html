How to be a (Roman) man

Closely concerned with philosophical approaches to dealing with grief and the fear of death, this treatise is also about what it means to be a man and how manhood can be forged in – and by – challenging circumstances. ‘For surely virtue [virtus] gives sufficient to make us live bravely; if bravely, sufficient too to make us high-souled and in fact never appalled by any event and always undefeated,’ he asserts. Similar concerns surface in other works he wrote around this time: in On Ends, he stresses the importance of self-control – which he defines as avoiding behaviour that’s ‘unmanly’ – parum virile.

Cicero’s philosophical writings were themselves an act of public service, he insisted. Through setting out philosophical ideas in Latin, he was offering a way for his fellow citizens to encounter the highest kind of learning. It was a particular comfort for Cicero in a time of deep political crisis, he declared, that this moment might see the birth of philosophy in Latin.
