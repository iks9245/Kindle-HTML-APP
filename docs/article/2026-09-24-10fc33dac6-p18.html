Strip away the physics from a quantum state, and the mathematical object you’re left with is a vector — an ordered list of numbers that acts like coordinates for a location in a mathematical space of possibilities. Quantum states are represented in a particular kind of vector space called a Hilbert space.

Vectors in Hilbert space follow specific rules. One of those rules says that any two states can be added together, and the result will also be a valid vector that, once normalized, will yield a valid quantum state. This is, mathematically, what quantum superposition gets at. States are coherent if they have neat phase relationships that allow them to interfere — adding up or canceling out, depending on how they line up. The coordinates of a vector in Hilbert space include information that corresponds to this alignment of peaks and troughs, called a wave’s phase.
