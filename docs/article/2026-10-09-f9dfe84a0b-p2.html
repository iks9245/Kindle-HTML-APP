The ghetto on the lagoon

On 29 March 1516, the Venetian Senate voted to create the world’s first ‘ghetto’. All Jews then living in the city – as well as any others who might come later – were ordered to move immediately to a small island in the northeastern sestiere of Cannaregio, a neighbourhood known as the geto novo. It was anything but appealing. Previously used as a dumping ground for the communal copper foundry, it had been converted into a residential quarter for local workers. From the outside, it already looked ‘like a castle’ – ringed by canals and bounded by perilously tall tenements. But by the time Venice’s Jews were herded in, it seemed more like a prison. All outward-facing doors and windows were blocked up; extra walls were built to close off access to the water; and a pair of sturdy iron gates were placed at either end of the bridge leading to Cannaregio. These were closed at sunset and opened in the morning at the ringing of the marangona bell. And just to be sure that no one could sneak out when they weren’t supposed to, four guards – paid for by the Jews themselves – patrolled the canals around the Ghetto ‘day and night’.
