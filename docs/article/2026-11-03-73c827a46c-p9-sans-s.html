小中大切換為襯線

The self-making world

The invertebrates, he told his students in 1800, ‘conduct us insensibly toward the inconceivable limit of animalisation’, toward those beings ‘barely endowed with animality’ with whom ‘nature perhaps began’. For Lamarck, this essence of animal life was the power of generation and regeneration: the ability to produce living matter, including new bodily tissues and new organisms. The simplest animals had the most widespread generative faculties. For instance, the Genevan naturalist Abraham Trembley had shown that, if you cut up polyps (freshwater hydras) into lots of pieces, they regenerate an entire animal from each part. Other invertebrates, too, had incredible powers of generation. Insects could metamorphose. Crustaceans could grow new legs. Sea stars, new rays. Snails, a new head. The invertebrates far outnumber the vertebrates, Lamarck explained, thanks to this generative ability. Their number is unknowably vast because of the ‘infinite smallness’ of the smallest among them. These animals display a ‘frightening fecundity’, he wrote; it seems that ‘matter animalises itself from every direction.’ Lamarck marvelled at the tiny coral polyps that were identified as the chief producers of the great calcareous mountain chains and enormous banks of chalk found all over Earth. These minuscule animals create islands, fill bays and gulfs, clog ports and ‘completely transform the coastlines’.
