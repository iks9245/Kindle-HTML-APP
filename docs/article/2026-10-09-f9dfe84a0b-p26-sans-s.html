The ghetto on the lagoon

The Ghetto was, from its inception, a site of exclusion. As the centuries wore on, efforts were repeatedly made to reinforce the barriers – both real and metaphorical – that separated its inhabitants from the Christian world beyond. Yet at no point was it ever hermetically sealed. Its very existence was founded on the necessity of exchange, interaction, even co-existence. The credit market, and later international trade, required there to be a constant to-ing and fro-ing between Jews and Christians. Exactly the same was true of cultural life. In a city as intellectually vibrant as Venice, where humanists were as fascinated by anything Hebrew as they were by Latin and Greek, the exchange of ideas was inevitable. And this is to say nothing of the fact that identities in early modern Venice were often so complicated that it was virtually impossible to separate any communities completely.
