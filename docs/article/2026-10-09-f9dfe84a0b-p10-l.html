Their success was hardly accidental. Since the mid-12th century, Christians had been forbidden to lend each other money at interest (a practice known as usury). To be sure, some enterprising souls had found a way round this – at least insofar as larger sums were concerned. Were a banker to lend a merchant 2,000 ducats to speculate on wood, for example, he could always dress up the loan as a ‘partnership’, and pretend that the interest was just his share of the profits. But smaller loans were another matter. If a sailor needed to borrow 2 ducats to buy food for his family, and agreed to pay it back, with a bit extra, a month later – well, that was usury, pure and simple. And that’s where the Jews of Mestre came in. As the Fourth Lateran Council had noted, usury was forbidden only between people of the same faith. Provided interest rates were not ‘excessive’, however, there was no reason why Jews couldn’t lend money to Christians. So, by lending small sums – the equivalent of today’s payday loans – the ebrei Tedeschi were able to fill a major gap. And the Venetian government was only too glad for them to do so. More likely than not, in 1366, several German Jews signed a contract (condotta) with the chief magistrate of Mestre, granting them protected status, in exchange for keeping their interest rates down.
