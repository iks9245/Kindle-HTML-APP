He was probably right

As Consul – the highest office in Republican Rome – in 63 BCE, he faced a conspiracy to overthrow the government led by a disgruntled senator, Lucius Sergius Catilina, who was in the field threatening Rome with an army. Cicero gathered what intelligence he could and acted on probabilia. He laid the evidence before the Senate, which passed the senatus consultum ultimum, an emergency decree authorising the consuls to take whatever measures the safety of the state required. Cicero then put the fate of the captured conspirators to a full senatorial debate, in which Caesar argued for imprisonment and Cato the Younger for death. On the constitutional understanding that Cicero and the Senate majority held, the executions that followed were lawful. That understanding was contested, then and since: citizens had a right of appeal, and several years later Cicero was driven into exile by a (retroactively applied) law framed precisely to assert that he had violated it. Nothing here settles whether he was right, and I am not arguing that he was. What is instructive is his method of arriving at a conclusion: he acted on a judgment he knew might be mistaken, claimed no certainty he did not have, and then spent the rest of his life defending the decision rather than disowning it. That is Academic Skepticism under fire – not paralysis, nor reckless certainty, but consequential commitment to the best available judgment, including its costs.
