Is There Anything Left in the Dream of Cold Fusion?

Although cold fusion was a pathological case, fusion in general has been a graveyard of dreams. I grew up on the dream it evoked. Whether it occurs at room temperature, as it was supposed to have done in the cold-fusion episode, or at center-of-the-sun temperatures, as in conventional thermonuclear reactors (and bombs), fusion doesn’t require strip-mining (and disposing of) uranium or escorting oil tankers through the Strait of Hormuz. All you would need, ideally, is a bit of seawater. Fascinated, I once built a “fusor” in my basement based on an article in Make magazine. Fusors are real, the one legitimate tabletop fusion technology. In them, a high voltage creates a hot plasma that smashes nuclei together. My unit didn’t achieve fusion, but with work it could have, although it would have produced negligible power. I loved showing guests its glowing purple plasma, but the more I learned about fusion, the more a sense of disillusionment grew in me. A couple of years later, a friend at a private fusion company gave me a tour of an actual experimental fusion reactor. It was complex and costly. So much for cheap. It produced intense, deadly neutron radiation. So much for clean. And it required tritium fuel, which does not occur naturally and has to be manufactured. So much for just a bit of seawater.
