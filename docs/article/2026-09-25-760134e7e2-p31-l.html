小中大切換為無襯線

Where a land ethic blooms

The leaves of big bluestem might otherwise have overshadowed a short-statured wild petunia seedling. Because the microbe/bison superorganism is adapted to make use of the abundant grass, the young wildflower now spends much of the summer bathed in light, taking in carbon dioxide, releasing oxygen, and building leaves and roots through photosynthesis. Furthermore, nitrogen in the big bluestem leaves would have been locked up through the rest of the growing season, unless a passing band of grasshoppers landed for a meal and carried it off, driven by the instinct to mate and lay eggs before the days become any shorter. As the bison eat and excrete, the nitrogen and other nutrients once held in the leaves overhead are returned in forms accessible to other community members. After pausing to scratch her belly on a compass plant, a bison cow returns a cascade of nitrogen-rich urine that the wildflower may absorb just as it begins to send its flowering stalk skyward.
