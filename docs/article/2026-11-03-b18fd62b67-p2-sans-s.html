Is There Anything Left in the Dream of Cold Fusion?

When the cold-fusion controversy erupted in 1989, I was in the middle of backpacking off the grid for six months. (You could do that back then.) By the time I caught up on the news, it was over. In March, two chemists had called a press conference to announce a simple tabletop experiment that released too much energy to be a mere chemical reaction. They said it had to be nuclear: hydrogen nuclei fusing to form helium. It promised limitless clean, cheap power. Physicists were stunned. That sort of thing wasn’t supposed to be possible under such pedestrian conditions. Politicians proclaimed the end of all our energy woes. But then labs across the globe tried to replicate the experiment and couldn’t, and an almighty takedown ensued. The whole thing only lasted a few months. Not having experienced the episode in real time, and being a contrarian by nature, I initially sympathized with the plucky maverick scientists. But then I read about their rookie mistakes, their fudging of a crucial graph, and their threats of lawsuits against skeptics. Experiments that initially corroborated the results were retracted, in one case amid indications of deliberate spiking with fake fusion products.
