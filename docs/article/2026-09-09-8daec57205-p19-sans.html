Our eloquent hands

The surprising thing is that some physicians put it precisely that way. It was initially strange to me that the London physician who observed the simian line on the Virgin Mary’s palm in the 1920s – Francis Crookshank – was also deeply schooled in the complexities of contemporary linguistics. Pondering the meaning of the simian line that some of his professional colleagues, as well as his patients, displayed, Crookshank began to insist that medicine desperately needed a ‘theory of signs’. One winter’s evening in 1926, he lectured on just that to the Royal College of Physicians and couldn’t understand why his medical brethren first rolled their eyes, and then closed their eyes, falling asleep with boredom. The connection he drew between clinical sign-reading and highly abstract semiotics was just too much. In so many ways, he was speaking a different language, and certainly his essay’s publication in the specialist book The Meaning of Meaning (1923) – to be read mainly by philosophers of language and Oxbridge cultural anthropologists – did nothing to persuade his medical colleagues. It may have been ill-delivered and badly pitched, but the fact is this palm-reading physician was theorising not just the simian line, but everyday clinical business: how signs on the human body are observed, named, and then ascribed meaning. He did so, not only, but not least for the simian line.
