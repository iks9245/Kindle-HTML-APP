Contemporary cognitive science of religion bears out this point. In a series of cross-cultural studies, researchers have found that, from a young age, people, even in religious societies like Iran and the US, report lower levels of confidence in the invisible religious entities they profess, like God and angels, than in invisible scientific entities, like germs and oxygen. That difference in confidence is presumably due to the felt poverty of evidence for the invisible religious entities. Yet people in some sense nonetheless ‘believe’ (read: religious credence) in the invisible religious entities. The psychologists Emily Liquin, S Emlen Metz and Tania Lombrozo conducted studies finding that participants indicated a lower need for explanation when it came to religious beliefs as opposed to scientific beliefs, while at the same time having a higher mystery acceptance for religious beliefs. So people are often fine treating their religious credences as inexplicable and mysterious.

That gives us our second difference between religious credence and matter-of-fact belief:

Difference number 2: factual belief is highly vulnerable to evidence, while religious credence is not vulnerable to evidence.
