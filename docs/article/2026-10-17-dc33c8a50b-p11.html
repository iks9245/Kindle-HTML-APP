Writing a century after Cicero, Quintilian was a huge admirer of his work, regarding him as the ideal model for the aspiring public speaker. In Quintilian’s wide-ranging treatise on the education of the ideal orator, sound ethical character but also manliness are presented as essential qualifications: ‘I am training the consummate orator, a figure whose existence is predicated on his being a good man [vir bonus].’ Quintilian expresses his disapproval of speeches composed merely for display, a widespread practice in his day, criticising teachers of rhetoric who encourage their students to show off:
