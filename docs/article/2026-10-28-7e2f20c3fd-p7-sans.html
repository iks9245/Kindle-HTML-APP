Part of what made the experience so disorienting was a narrow idea of what grief is. In psychology, in philosophy and in our Western culture at large, grief has been treated almost exclusively as a response to the death of someone we love. It’s a neat, tidy understanding. The most celebrated grief memoirs – think of The Year of Magical Thinking (2005) by Joan Didion and A Grief Observed (1961) by C S Lewis – are concerned with the loss of a life partner. And Michael Cholbi’s Grief: A Philosophical Guide (2021) – one of the most extensive philosophical treatments of the emotion – focuses mostly on the grief we experience at the death of someone we love or who otherwise matters to us.

Restricting grief to bereavement is like restricting pain to cluster headaches
