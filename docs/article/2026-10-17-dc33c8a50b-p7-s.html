How to be a (Roman) man

Orators were alert to the niceties of their own – and one another’s – masculine self-fashioning. In the cacophonous world of the Roman republic, politicians, advocates and those aspiring to a public role exchanged no-holds-barred insults. Often, they cast aspersions on one another’s masculinity, with disparaging comments on an opponent’s way of speaking, his gestures, the way he wore his toga – as well as his sexual behaviour. Orators would be praised for speaking in a ‘manly’ way. And they were robustly attacked for any ‘effeminate’ deviations in their style and manner of speech. Some orators favoured a more elaborate style fashionable in the long-established rhetorical schools of the eastern Mediterranean, the zone the Romans called Asia. Contrasts were drawn between ‘Asian’ style and the plainer style associated with Athenian rhetorical tradition. The Asian style was regularly denigrated as ‘effeminate’. Yet too ‘unpolished’ a style could seem old-fashioned, even coarse. Moderation was itself celebrated as a quintessentially masculine quality. But where to draw the line?
