Reason is more than a tool

The Frankfurt School saw a version of this danger, too, and long before machine learning came along. Writing in exile during the Second World War, as fascism, antisemitism, Stalinism and monopoly capitalism shattered 19th-century hopes for steady moral progress, Max Horkheimer, Theodor Adorno and others asked how the Enlightenment’s promise of liberation could collapse into new forms of domination. How could societies committed to the promise of science and reason give rise to such destruction and terror? The problem, they found, was not reason itself, but reason narrowed into mere instrumentality, the capacity to classify, predict, administer, dominate and control, decoupled from any notion of human dignity. Reason so conceived – as an intelligent servant – readily becomes, when enacted at scale, an instrument of barbarity.
