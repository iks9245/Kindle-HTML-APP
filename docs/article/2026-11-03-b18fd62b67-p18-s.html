And that’s important because, although physicists first observed muon-catalyzed fusion in the 1950s, few studied it, deeming it a mere curiosity. What good could it be if it couldn’t generate energy? The main development of recent years has not been a breakthrough on muon recycling but a realization that we don’t need any such breakthrough.

I think that carries a general lesson for evaluating developments in science. What makes headlines — Abundant energy! Humanity saved! — isn’t what you should focus on. Instead, ask: Will this research program be interesting, even if the main claim falls short? Is it an all-or-nothing gamble on a single result, or a portal into a wider area of science?

Cold fusion, as presented to the world in 1989, failed this test. Those two chemists never articulated why their experiment might be interesting even if it didn’t herald energy salvation. That task fell to Trevithick 30 years later, when his project showed that a cold-fusion type of experiment, if pursued on its own merits, could reveal fascinating material properties.
