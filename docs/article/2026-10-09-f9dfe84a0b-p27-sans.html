The ghetto on the lagoon

It was not long before a new ‘solution’ presented itself. On 23 April 1515 – 18 months after the Venetian defeat at the Battle of Schio – Giorgio Emo, an important member of the Collegio – the nearest thing Venice had to a cabinet – proposed that the Jews should be confined to the island of Giudecca. It seemed to be a reasonable compromise. There, Emo argued, the Jews would be kept far enough away from the Christian population to prevent religious ‘infection’ from spreading, yet close enough that they could keep the wheels of finance turning. Naturally, the Jews were appalled. They protested that, if the Jewish community was forced to move to Giudecca, it would be in danger of being attacked by the mercenaries billeted there. They therefore suggested the island of Murano instead. But when this, too, turned out to be unworkable, Emo’s proposal was quietly shelved. The principle of segregation was not in any doubt, though. It was just that, for the moment, the government didn’t have the time – or the inclination – to find a more suitable location.
