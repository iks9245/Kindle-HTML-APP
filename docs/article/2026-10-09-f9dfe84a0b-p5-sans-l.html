This was no small matter. Before then, Jewish quarters had been known by all sorts of different names (Judenstraße, Judengasse, Juiverie, etc). But none of them had ever broken through into common parlance as a way of describing segregation per se. They were either too closely associated with a specific geographical feature – a street or a square – or so imprecise that they could refer to just about anything. ‘Ghetto’ offered something different. Precisely because the word was being used metonymically, rather than just geographically, the word ‘ghetto’ could be used to describe any Jewish segregation anywhere. And soon enough, that’s exactly what happened. In around 1562, people started calling the Jewish quarter in Rome a ‘ghetto’; and less than 10 years later, another ‘ghetto’ was established in Florence. And so it went on – right through the 17th century, down to the eve of the French Revolution.
