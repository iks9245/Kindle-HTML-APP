Dolphins and other lovers

Amazonians know desire well: desire is what the rainforest is all about. Those who live far from the Amazon tend to see the food chain as its governing metaphor, but, for those who live inside the forest, the boundaries between eating and mating blur. It is no wonder that, for an Andoque hunter, dreaming of a beautiful woman signals an encounter with forest meat, while a dream about sex foretells illness. The Amazon rainforest is, at its core, a sexual space: 2.3 million square miles of desire, where survival depends on consuming others – culinarily or sexually – while living in constant fear of being consumed yourself. For Amazonians, the world is first and foremost a huge pool of potential partners and, by marrying them, an even larger pool of potential in-laws who would become allies. Even animals are drawn into this logic. For many Indigenous groups, sex and predation are two forms through which the same mode of relation materialises. The consummation of ‘marriage’ with animals may therefore take the form either of consuming their meat or of being consumed by them through fatal attacks. As anthropologists like Eduardo Viveiros de Castro and Aparecida Vilaça have shown, within this chaotic tangle of desire and survival, family – the people with whom the urge to consume or mate is deliberately restrained – must be actively created by sharing food, care and knowledge. In the Amazonian worldview, attraction is the rule, isolation the exception.
