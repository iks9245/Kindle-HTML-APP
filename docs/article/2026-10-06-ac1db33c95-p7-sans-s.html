Jerne’s unusual candour about his personal and intimate life led me, early on, to view his private and scientific realms as an integrated whole. Most of my colleagues in the history of science viewed biography as an ancilla historiae – a handmaid to history – and thus focused on the social and cultural context of researchers and their work. In contrast, I chose to focus on the personal context, developing an approach I termed existential scientific biography. As a lifelong admirer of Søren Kierkegaard, Jerne enthusiastically embraced this framework.

Jerne died in 1994. By the time my book about him, Science as Autobiography, appeared in 2003, I had been appointed professor of the history of medicine at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark. There, I hoped to focus on medical biography and autobiography. But I quickly became absorbed in transforming the university’s medical history museum into Medical Museion. My life-writing went on standby for several years.
