In praise of the dilettante

Crucially, Goethe and Schiller’s analysis is squarely situated in the cultural context of Weimar classicism. This means that, first, the question of the dilettante arises (as it does in its original Italian usage) exclusively in relation to the arts. They treat the dilettante as a distinctly aesthetic figure, ‘a lover of the arts who wishes not only to contemplate and enjoy them, but also to participate in their production.’ This aesthetic dimension has (regrettably, I think) faded from the meaning of ‘dilettante’ over time. But nor does it need to be narrowly inscribed in 18th-century anxieties about who can and cannot properly be called an artist. Artistic engagement isn’t essential to dilettantism. But it is worth reasserting, maybe even foregrounding, the sense in which the ‘delight’ of dilettantism involves, to some extent, the experience of aesthetic pleasure, broadly understood. The dilettante, that is, pursues what Søren Kierkegaard calls an ‘aesthetic life’: the ongoing, everyday activity of observing, valuing, and participating in art and beauty.
