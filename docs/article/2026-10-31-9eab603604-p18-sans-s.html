In praise of the dilettante

Also crucial to making this mental switch is seeing the dilettante’s curiosity as equal in intensity to the expert’s, but different. Picture the dilettante as the love-child of two very different philosophical figures: Kierkegaard’s aesthete in Either/Or (1843) who finds and cultivates interests with an intensity that is abandoned before it hardens into obligation, and Friedrich Nietzsche’s ‘free spirit’, the fiercely curious, light-footed nomad whose restless pursuit of intellectual and artistic treasures provides a profound, if rootless, freedom. From Kierkegaard, the dilettante acquires an innate aversion to boredom (‘the root of all evil’) which is achieved by what he calls ‘rotation of crops’: perpetually changing ‘the method of cultivation and the kinds of crops’. From Nietzsche, the dilettante inherits a rejection of convention coupled with perpetual motion, a sense of permanent ‘homelessness’ that propels an unfettered perusal of interests. Passionate enough to go deep, detached enough to eventually leave, such a figure shows the possibility of being intensely invested in various ways of knowing, doing and making, but never content to let a sense of self form too firmly around them.
