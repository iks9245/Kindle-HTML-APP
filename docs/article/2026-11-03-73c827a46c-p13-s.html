小中大切換為無襯線

The self-making world

When a seal arrived in the menagerie of the natural history museum in June 1809, Lamarck watched the animal with fascination. Its obvious kinship with land mammals confirmed his idea that all mammals, and indeed all life, had originated in the water. He noticed that the seal used its hind limbs as a paddle when swimming. Yet the legs hadn’t fully joined together to become a fin because the seal also used them for seizing prey. The distinctive combination of uses for the hind limbs had produced a structure that could work either like a paddle or like a hand.

Lamarck had set Aristotle’s ancient Scale of Nature not just in motion but in a great commotion. For two millennia, since the time of Aristotle, natural philosophers had imagined every being ranked from simplest to most complex in a fixed, unchanging order. Each form of life had its appointed place in the hierarchy. But now Lamarck announced that, rather than being arrayed along the Scale of Nature, living beings were themselves ever making and remaking the order of life by their own incessant activity, and in many directions at once.
