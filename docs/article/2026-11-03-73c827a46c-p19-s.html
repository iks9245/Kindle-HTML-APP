A founding figure in this movement was the German biologist August Weismann. In the 1880s, he began to draw a sharp distinction between somatic (body) cells and germ (reproductive) cells. He said that no changes taking place in the somatic cells could ever be transmitted to the germ cells. This is the so-called Weismann barrier, eliminating the possibility of ‘inheritance of acquired characters’. In a rather cruel series of experiments, Weismann chopped the tails off several generations of mice in order to show that their offspring had perfectly healthy tails. He himself acknowledged that this result had no real bearing on Lamarck’s theory: neither Lamarck nor Darwin had ever claimed that amputations were inherited, only internally generated changes. But Weismann reckoned it would defeat popular belief in the inheritance of acquired characteristics. The mouse-tail experiments remain rhetorically powerful, and still appear in biology textbooks today.
