小中大切換為襯線

The bombarding of childhood

The shows scoring highest on the slow rubric are those that leave room around the characters’ actions and allow the child to recognise the actions. Often there is a face, a puppet, a song, a greeting, a familiar body on screen that treats attention as a relationship. Indeed, the power of programmes such as the US show Mister Rogers’ Neighborhood (1968-2001), the Norwegian series Fantorangen (2007-), and the British shows In the Night Garden… (2007-09) and Teletubbies (1997-2001) lies in their ability to build a rapport with the child and then repeat, almost as in a ritual. Here, small acts and greetings or songs provide a familiar frame, almost like a safety net, within which new concepts can be introduced. In the Australian animated series Bluey (2018-24), play often begins in an ordinary family situation and develops into imaginative games that involve parents, siblings and friends; the pace can be lively, but it tends to return the child to pretend play, negotiation and emotional understanding. In the animated TV series The Adventures of Paddington (2019-25) – a British-French co-production – gentle mishaps are usually held inside a world of curiosity, politeness, family and repair, so comedy becomes a route into perseverance or social understanding. Such programmes are slow because they leave openings through which the story can travel back into meaning and play.
