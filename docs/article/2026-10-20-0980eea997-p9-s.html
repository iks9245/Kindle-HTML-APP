小中大切換為無襯線

Horrible, unmanly, indispensable

By the start of 1539, Duke Cosimo de’ Medici owned four wheellock handguns made in the German style that he (or more likely his bodyguards) took with him when he travelled. Not a decade on, a wheellock appeared in the first portrait of a European ruler with a handgun: Titian’s equestrian portrait of the victorious Emperor Charles V after the Battle of Mühlberg, in which Charles is shown ‘in the manner in which he went against the rebels’, that is, in the garb of the wheellock-toting Spanish cavalry.

The aesthetic qualities of high-end firearms helped guns blend into the wider courtly environment. Designs echoed imagery common to the court: hunting scenes, battles, motifs from ancient history and myth, or biblical figures like Judith. A wheellock pistol made in Germany and dated 1548 features a figure of the Roman heroine Lucretia along with a Christian motto and image of the Crucifixion. By this time, guns were finding new uses in Europe’s religious wars: far from the Devil’s weapon, they were a means of asserting true Christianity against the heretics.
