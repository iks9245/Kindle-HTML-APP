The ancient Romans discovered this in their amphitheatres. Roman chariot races didn’t come without their headaches. Or rather, foot aches. I’m certain the drivers likely complained about their hot feet, caused by friction between the wheels and axle heating up the surrounding parts. In Odes, a collection of lyric poems, the poet Horace described how chariot wheels would glow during racing. Today, scientists know that this glow is from turning the energy of motion into thermal energy. Roman designers eventually figured out that they could temper the problem by putting axungia (animal fat) grease between the wheel and axle. By doing so, they were applying tribological knowledge already exploited by the Sumerians: that fats and oils work more effectively than water as lubrication between surfaces. However, that created a new conundrum: if the grease got hot enough, it risked catching fire. The solution? A coolant. In this case, they splashed water on the chariots, in the ancient equivalent of a pit stop.
