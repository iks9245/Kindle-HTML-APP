Dolphins and other lovers

Yoóka stand in contrast with bɨ’iá, ‘working tools’, which contribute to people’s prosperity and wellbeing: the very same alcohol can be bɨ’iá if it brings people together; the machete when it helps you open paths in the forest; the rifle when a successful hunt enables you to feed your family; and foreign goods when they sustain rather than undermine life. As the gruesome story of the enchanted boat that lured away the Cacambra clan recounts, deviation by yoóka is where the dolphin merges with the fi’du; where consuming the dazzle and convenience of non-Indigenous goods leads to being consumed by the forest. Living in the unforgiving Amazon, you constantly ward off blood-sucking mosquitoes, carry a machete just to bathe safely lest a venomous snake crosses your path, and witness vines strangling trees whose rotting corpses nourish new growth. Every time you see a shaman or elder ‘healing’ meat through chants before giving it to a child or a woman giving birth, you are reminded of the vengeful intentions of the guardian spirits of animals. In this zero-sum game between humans and nonhumans, it is little wonder that forest dwellers who become ‘white’ expose themselves to the jungle’s predatory forces.
