I rebelled. Surely there was something between atomisation – what the philosopher Charles Taylor calls the ‘buffered self’ – and hierarchical, authoritarian community; between girl-boss and trad-wife. The final push to try and find it, ultimately, was fear. When my first child was born, the reality of the climate crisis moved from a theoretical policy problem to one that bubbled acidly in my belly, settled itself in my bones. I had brought this powerless being into a world on fire, and the idea that hard work and individual accumulation could keep her safe stopped making sense. The need to be part of a thick web of humans who would not play a passing role in each other’s lives but commit to costly mutual care felt urgent as the ground bucked and roiled beneath our feet. I wanted ‘a village’, as we say now, not for the golden Instagram images of laughing friends around a photogenic table, but because, in my bleakest moments, I thought it might be essential for survival.
