小中大切換為無襯線

The self-making world

Neo-Darwinism, with its passive model of living beings, has been teetering on its throne. One current movement, known as the extended evolutionary synthesis, seeks to widen the evolutionary picture beyond the neo-Darwinist focus on random genetic variation and natural selection, in part by including behavioural and even cultural elements. Many animals have something like what we call culture in humans, and this includes cultural forms that are inherited and passed down. Lamarck knew this. He wrote that intelligent animals – birds and mammals – could create new habits and ways of life that would be maintained over generations. There is overwhelming and definitive evidence that all kinds of animals – birds, primates, whales, fish, insects – learn from one another and have cultural traditions that are handed down from generation to generation. Communication and social learning make a crucial difference to survival and reproduction, and therefore to evolution. Animals communicate vitally important things to one another including information about food, predators, songs, migration routes and where to find a mate. To give just one example, humpback whales develop different approaches to hunting from one community to another – one in the Northeast Pacific, another in the Gulf of Maine – and teach these to their offspring.
