The physics of getting fired

For me, the real value of physics had never been as a cudgel for smashing things up into their smallest bits, but as a framework for navigating systems – for recognising patterns, probing assumptions, and testing whether the models we rely on actually work as they’re meant to. We see this way of thinking throughout the history of science. When cholera swept through Soho in 1854, the physician John Snow didn’t know its cause. But he mapped the cases, identified the pattern, and traced the outbreak to a single water pump on Broad Street. The founder of what we now know as epidemiology, Snow worked by constructing and interrogating mental models of the world, probing their assumptions, and following the data wherever they led.

The question, then, is not simply whether physics can be borrowed as rhetoric, but whether it really helps us to see the world more clearly, in ways that complement and benefit other disciplines. That, at least, was what stayed with me after I stopped studying it formally. Not equations or textbooks, but a way of thinking that I found myself reaching for when my world was falling apart.
