Reasoning together

I understood almost none of it at first: words I’d read were coming to life in the room, spoken by their author, and still not resolving into sense. After several lectures, the shape came into focus: this wasn’t just Between Facts and Norms being rebuilt in public, but decades of work arriving at a single place. Since The Theory of Communicative Action (1981), Habermas had been arguing that reason survives in modern life only as something people do together, that is, by giving each other reasons and staying open to being moved by them, rather than by force, money or tradition. Between Facts and Norms was that idea turned toward law and democracy directly: a state counts as legitimate only when its laws could in principle be justified to everyone bound by them, through exactly that kind of open exchange. I’d watched that argument take root in a decades-long intellectual history. Thinking is a practice, it involves owing each other explanations, questions, answers. It is not a fast process, and doing it in public, out loud, in front of a room, is what makes it worthwhile.
