How to be a (Roman) man

The collapse of the Roman state in its traditional form was something Cicero had been lamenting, at least on and off, for some years. He often used the language of death, bereavement and mourning in relation to the political situation. ‘For a long time it has been my part to mourn our liberties,’ he wrote to his old friend Atticus. Just a short while before Tullia’s death, he wrote to another friend: ‘I have already grieved for the state more deeply and longer than any mother for her only child.’ It is impossible to disentangle Cicero’s devastation at the loss of his daughter from his feelings of loss at the drastic transformation of Rome’s political landscape. The one loss intensified his grief at the other. And as Cicero’s words comparing himself to a bereaved mother suggest, he experienced both the loss and the intense grief it provoked as profoundly emasculating. He could no longer be the man he once was.
