Reason is more than a tool

Yet the fact that instrumental reason can serve domination does not mean that Kant’s richer conception of reason offers any automatic safeguard against evil. Nothing does. The language of Kantian universalism has itself been used to discipline, exclude and humiliate, as if some people were more fully rational, more capable of autonomy, more entitled to speak in the name of humanity than others. But that is not an objection to Kant’s conception of reason. It is an objection to its betrayal. A view of reason that is used to silence or instrumentalise others contradicts itself, because reason is not a private weapon but a common tribunal – the space in which claims and ends must become answerable to all, as equal bearers of dignity. That is precisely what the Humean servant model cannot say. If reason is only an instrument for satisfying antecedent ends, then it cannot condemn domination as irrational or at odds with human dignity. After all, if it is not contrary to reason ‘to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger’, then reason has already surrendered the authority Kant most wanted to defend, which is our ability to judge not only how we pursue our ends, but whether those ends are worthy of us in the first place.
