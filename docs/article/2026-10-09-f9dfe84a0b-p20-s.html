The ghetto on the lagoon

This last clause was probably just meant to be a sweetener. Although Venice was struggling on the battlefield, things were not so bad that anyone seriously expected the bankers of Mestre to flee. But, before long, all that would change. Following a catastrophic defeat at the Battle of Agnadello on 14 May 1509, Venice’s control over the terraferma suddenly collapsed. As French troops advanced on the lagoon, refugees began to stream into the city – including hundreds of Jews from Mestre and other subject cities. The bankers who had signed the 1503 condotta came with the government’s blessing, but a great many more did not.

Terrified that this sudden influx of people would cause a shortage of food, the Venetian government tried desperately to expel anyone who did not have permission to stay. But the frightened refugees refused to budge – and no amount of cajoling could persuade them to do otherwise. Quite suddenly, Venice found itself confronted with precisely the Jewish presence it had tried for so long to avoid.
