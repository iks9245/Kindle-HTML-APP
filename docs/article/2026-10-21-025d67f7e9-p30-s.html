How intuitions work

The person who declines to get in the stranger’s car, or who flags the email as a phishing attempt, or who walks away from the deal – these individuals are not acting irrationally or on mere feeling. They are responding to a genuine presentational state: something is showing up as creepy, fraudulent or wrong. The situation is presenting itself as having a certain character, and that presentation carries epistemic weight – not because it is infallible, but because it is the same kind of state that grounds all of our most basic cognitive contact with the world.

This is precisely what philosophers mean when they talk about intuition. The difference between the person on the bridge who immediately senses that pushing the bystander is wrong, and the person who declines to get into a stranger’s car, is not a difference in kind. It is a difference in context and control. The philosopher’s thought experiment is a deliberately engineered situation designed to isolate and interrogate a single intuitive response. The everyday case is messier, embedded in a richer context, and rarely examined. But the underlying capacity, the presentational apprehension of how things are, is the same.
