小中大切換為無襯線

Malaria Parasite Caught in the Act of Breaking and Entering

For example, Plasmodium invades human red blood cells as part of its life cycle. To get in, the microbe creates a ring-shaped structure called the moving junction, which briefly creates a gap in the cell membrane it can slip through. The gap then disappears without a trace. It opens and closes so rapidly, at such a small scale, that imaging technologies couldn’t capture it. Researchers had no clue what the portal structure actually looked like. “Because of that,” said Chi-Min Ho, a microbiologist and structural biologist at Columbia University, “we didn’t really know anything about how it works.”

Now, Ho’s lab has imaged the moving junction right at the moment of invasion, in unprecedented detail. Her team found that when the Plasmodium parasite installs molecular gaskets in the host cell, it radically remodels the cell membrane to slip through without a trace. This knowledge could help inform the next generation of malaria treatments and influence the drug-discovery process for other parasitic diseases, such as cyclosporiasis and toxoplasmosis.
