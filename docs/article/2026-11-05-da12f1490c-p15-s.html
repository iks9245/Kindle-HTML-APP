He and his colleagues argue that tests of introspection in language models should take inspiration from the leading theories of how introspection works in humans. In one prominent theory, the defining feature of introspection is that it’s based on particular mental representations of other thoughts. According to this view, thinking about apples involves a direct, or “first-order,” mental representation of the concept “apples,” while introspective awareness of your own thoughts involves a “second-order” mental representation, such as “I’m thinking about apples.” But when it comes to how concepts are represented in a language model — or a human brain, for that matter — it’s surprisingly tricky to draw a sharp distinction between first-order and second-order representations.

“Going down to a mechanistic level and probing the internals of a large language model makes you realize that that distinction is actually quite fuzzy, and maybe it’s fuzzy in humans as well,” said Steve Fleming, a neuroscientist at University College London who studies introspection in humans. “Cognitive scientists were in this illusory comfort zone.”
