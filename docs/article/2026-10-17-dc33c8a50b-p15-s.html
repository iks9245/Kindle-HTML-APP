How to be a (Roman) man

Cicero, most eloquent exponent of Roman republican values, struggled to articulate his profound political disappointment. This was an experience compounded by devastating personal bereavement. In 45 BCE, shortly after giving birth to a little boy, Cicero’s daughter Tullia died. He was knocked sideways. Friends wrote letters of consolation. A long letter from one, Servius Sulpicius Rufus, observed that at least Tullia, dying when she did, avoided witnessing the end of the republic. In an equally lengthy reply, Cicero acknowledged his friend’s kindness – but disputed the claim that any comfort might be found in the timing of Tullia’s death. He contrasted his own situation with that of earlier Roman public figures, who had managed to avoid giving in to personal griefs by devoting themselves to public service: ‘the high standing they derived from public life soothed their mourning’. In current political circumstances, Cicero no longer had a part in public life. He couldn’t bear even the sight of the senate – the scene of his former triumphs. In these wretched political times, it was Tullia who had been Cicero’s one source of comfort. She was ‘a haven of refuge and repose, one in whose conversation and sweet ways I put aside all cares and sorrows.’ And now she was gone.
