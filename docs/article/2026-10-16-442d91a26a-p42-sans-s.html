KIERS: Yeah, this is my PhD work, focused on the ways in which soybeans, so legumes, are able to discriminate and actually punish. Here we use the word punish, their nitrogen-fixing bacteria if they fail to provide nitrogen. So I can give you some context about these manipulative experiments that we set up with soybeans and bacteria. We really wanted to understand how plants prevent these bacteria from taking too much resources. Again, when you’ve got a very intimate symbiosis, there’s always a chance that the microbe itself can turn into a parasite and just start to consume resources from inside. And just to take a step back, legumes are this incredible family of plants in nature that form nodules that are full of something called rhizobia, which are nitrogen-fixing bacteria.

And these bacteria are incredible. They take nitrogen from the air, and they break that really strong triple bond, and they put it into a form of nitrogen that the plant can use. So they’re actually making fertilizer out of the air. It’s absolutely incredible what they can do, but it’s really expensive.
