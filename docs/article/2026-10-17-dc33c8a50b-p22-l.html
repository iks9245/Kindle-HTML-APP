And yet perhaps another sphere of activity might offer scope for a new kind of self-actualisation, a new way for a man of Cicero’s extraordinary talents to win fame through service to the state and his fellow Romans. Even before Tullia’s death, Cicero had been increasingly immersing himself in philosophy. In 46 BCE, he had written to Sulpicius of his retreat from public-speaking: ‘seeing there is no further place in the Senate House or Forum for the craft which I have studied, I have devoted all my time and attention to philosophy.’ In changed political circumstances, with the dictator Caesar as Rome’s sole ruler, philosophy felt like a kind of salvation.
