How to be a (Roman) man

By the time he secured the consulship, Cicero was regarded as Rome’s top orator. Public speaking was highly prized by members of Rome’s governing elite. Its end result mattered hugely – winning over one’s listeners. But the way Rome’s politicians spoke and even how they dressed could also serve to demonstrate their manliness. Codes of civilian masculinity governed behaviour in public spaces such as the Forum, the law-courts and the senate. Markers of manliness in this sphere were subject to relentless public scrutiny. As the later expert on oratory Quintilian put it, the orator’s dress needs to be ‘resplendent and manly: for when it comes to one’s toga and shoes and hair, excessive care as well as negligence are equally blameable’. The toga, for instance, must be correctly draped; he notes a little later, ‘throwing back the fold from its bottom onto the right shoulder is foppish and effeminate’.
