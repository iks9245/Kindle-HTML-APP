Other people

To date, we have lived up close – bodily fluids close – with people we are not related to for nearly six years. We share all the space in our jointly mortgaged home except the bedrooms. We pool most of our money, cook, shop, pray and host hundreds of guests a year, together. And we manage countless petty conflicts, over the milk or the dishwasher or the crumbs on the side, conflicts that often mask deeper questions: who do I belong to? Where can I be really at home, be really myself? Who am I becoming, alongside these people? And are we able, when it comes down to it, to be each other’s safety?

There are very few models for what we are doing. Perhaps the most robust is the monastic tradition. Most orders of religious Sisters or Brothers see the proximity of living up close with each other as essential to spiritual growth. It is precisely in the discomfort of being witnessed, up close, at our worst, in being asked to compromise preferences, to share and serve others, they argue, that we really grow up.
