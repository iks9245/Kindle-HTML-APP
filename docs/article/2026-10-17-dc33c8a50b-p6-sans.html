How to be a (Roman) man

When Cicero was 16, like others of his social standing, his transition to adulthood was marked by a special ceremony, most likely at the feast of the Liberalia in 90 BCE. This was the moment when he first put on the toga of manhood, a cumbersome length of woollen fabric worn by Roman citizen men, a garment associated with civilian as opposed to military life. Years later, as consul in 63 BCE, Cicero played a leading role in uncovering and suppressing a conspiracy against the Roman state, and boasted that this was a victory won by ‘citizens in togas’. It was his speeches that had convinced his fellow senators and the Roman people of the dangers posed by Catiline’s conspiracy, leading to decisive action against those involved. Cicero had used his voice as consul to save Rome, he declared in a speech a few years later. Throughout his life, he insisted his victory was comparable to those of Rome’s greatest generals. Masculine achievements weren’t confined to the battlefield; rhetoric and oratory could also conquer.
