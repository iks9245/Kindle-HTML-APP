How to be a (Roman) man

In the second Philippic, Cicero insisted at length and in colourful detail on the ways in which Antony was a slave to bodily pleasures – a man with no capacity for self-control. Cicero dredged up a story from years previously alleging Antony had had a homosexual affair with another Roman elite man, Curio, a relationship in which Antony willingly played a ‘passive’ role: ‘No slave boy bought to satisfy lust was ever so completely in his master’s power as you were in Curio’s,’ he fulminated. The lust here is implicitly Curio’s, not Antony’s – but this willingness to subordinate himself revealed Antony’s ‘slavish’ disposition. Such sexual ‘passivity’ in an elite male – taking what was seen as a ‘feminine’, receptive sexual role – was deeply shocking for Romans. It is notable that in his swansong Cicero does his utmost to paint Antony as a man of ‘unmanly’ sexual habits, addicted to pleasure and with no sense of masculine moderation. Cicero, ever the rhetorician out to win the argument, was quite prepared to exploit his audience’s masculinist prejudices to demean an enemy.
