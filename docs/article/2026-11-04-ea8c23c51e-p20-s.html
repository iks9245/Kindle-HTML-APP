Furthermore, such linguistic differentiation has parallels in other languages, including Fante (a dialect of the Akan people of Ghana), Thai, Mandarin and Bislama (a creole spoken in Vanuatu). Further studies show that people’s use of the word ‘believes’ correlates with seeing an attitude as being held voluntarily and not for evidential reasons – for example, out of loyalty – and as giving rise to symbolic action, like public pronouncements. People’s use of the word ‘thinks’, on the other hand, correlates with seeing an attitude as being held for evidential reasons and as giving rise to truth-dependent instrumental action (like buying property or making investments). All of that suggests that most people are at some level tracing the differences that the foregoing theory makes explicit – and they’re often using the words ‘believe’ versus ‘think’ to report religious credence versus factual belief.

Let’s return to Jefferson’s idea and to the political realm more generally. How does the psychological difference between factual belief and religious credence support the political principle of separating church and state? We think that understanding the difference – psychological dynamics and all – helps us see two justifications that we couldn’t have articulated clearly otherwise.
