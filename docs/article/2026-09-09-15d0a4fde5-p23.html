But it was migrants, not tourists, who became the primary long-distance users of the Pan-American Highway and its tributaries. Despite motel construction and flashy campaigns promoting national scenic sites, automotive tourists never showed up in significant numbers on the road. This had less to do with the quality or unfinished status of the highway than with its extraordinary length. Boosters touted the highway as the world’s longest, expecting that moniker alone to draw motorists toward distant adventures, but the Pan-American Highway proved too long for the average American’s two-week paid vacation. Reporters imagined Ohio suburbanites driving to Rio de Janeiro as if on a whim. But a drive to South America, even without the inevitable hassle of shipping one’s car around the Darién Gap, was a commitment of many months, even if going just one way. The few who did get as far as Panama typically sold their cars to US military personnel in the Canal Zone and sailed or flew home because they could not face the hardship of driving all the way back.
