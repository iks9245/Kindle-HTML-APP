Life on a hair trigger

The idea that changing how we see can change how we respond is ancient. In the 2nd century, Epictetus said: ‘Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of them.’ What is unusual is its application. Offering the opportunity to reframe a situation, as a serious answer to lethal violence, to a 26-year-old sitting in his room with a loaded clip is a novel proposition. It begins from an assumption too rarely extended to men like King with a history of violence: that despite what he has been through, his inner life is not fixed.

READI offered men a chance to discipline their inner worlds. The 18-month intervention paired a part-time job with cognitive behavioural sessions, held three to five mornings a week, that participants called ‘CAD’ (Control-Alt-Delete, the keystroke that reboots a computer). The lessons taught participants how to slow down to reconsider an automatic reaction and widen the range of responses in a tense moment. They learned that they did not have to be ruled by their first feeling or thought. In doing so, they began to loosen the identity that violence had formed
