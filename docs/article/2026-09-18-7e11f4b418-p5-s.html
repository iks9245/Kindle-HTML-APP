The chefs were hired to help program the ovens to follow their recipes. But they also had to pretend they were normal people cooking dinner for their families in order to see if the ovens could replicate expert techniques. On one hand, the chefs needed an interface with enough controls to make manual adjustments, adding to the oven’s corpus of training data. On the other hand, end users would need simplicity and the impression of smarts.

The company building the ovens viewed human intervention in the recipes as a liability. Having too many options would invite users to fiddle with the recipes, which would cut against the ovens’ smarts and risk producing a bad result. The product team talked a lot about users forming ‘bad habits’ if they manually adjusted recipes too much. ‘Change the ritual’ became a mantra written on whiteboards, sticky notes and task trackers around the office.

An oven can’t taste. It has no idea what ‘smacking’ something with a propane torch means
