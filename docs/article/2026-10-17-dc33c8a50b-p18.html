How to be a (Roman) man

Back in Rome, however, Cicero remained anxious. Generals in command of large armies – Julius Caesar, above all – asserted their dominance over the state. Rome’s senate felt increasingly powerless. ‘The republic is an empty husk,’ Cicero lamented in a letter to Atticus in 54 BCE. Another celebrated general, Pompey, eventually led the challenge to bring Caesar under control. Yet, even if this was an attempt to shore up traditional modes of government where the senate exercised greatest authority, it ended in failure – and there were also doubts about Pompey’s intentions. Was he too, perhaps, aiming at one-man rule? Following a bitter civil war lasting from 49 to 45 BCE, a victorious Julius Caesar ruled the Roman world as dictator. Cicero, Rome’s foremost orator and long at the centre of its political life, was in despair. Throughout his career, he had championed a traditional political order in which Rome’s elite competed against one another in elections and took collective decisions in the senate. This was above all a culture of robust debate, in which persuasive speech played a critical role. But now the Forum was silent. In the senate, no one dared speak against Caesar.
