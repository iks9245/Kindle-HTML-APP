That was just the beginning. After Jewish segregation was abolished in Italy, in the wake of the Napoleonic invasions, the word ‘ghetto’ came to be applied even more widely. Now that its link with a specific type of Jewish segregation was broken, it could denote many more varieties of confinement, marginalisation and exclusion. Brought to the United States in the late 19th century, it was used to describe the poverty and precarity of any migrant group. And after the Nazi invasion of Poland in September 1939, it was applied with terrifying potency to sites of captivity and genocide. Even today, its meanings continue to multiply. Open a newspaper or shuffle your playlist, and you’ll find it used to stigmatise an ‘other’ – or, more often, to reclaim a lost power. And though all these ‘ghettos’ are very different, they all trace their origins back to Venice.
