A life in episodes

I once caught myself doing the very thing the open feuilleton is supposed to protect against – and the form saved me from myself. Something I had carried as private shame for most of my academic life was a compulsion to raise my hand at conferences and seminars. After one such meeting in 1979, I had written in my diary that, as usual, I could not restrain myself, giving in to a ‘pathological drive’ to dominate the room with my questions: ‘I am conditioned to the moment of questioning a speaker. I cannot keep my mouth shut. My heart pounds; I feel an irresistible compulsion … What is this inner need to show myself off in company?’ The pattern was unmistakable: wherever I sat – a hundred people in the room, time for only two questions – I had to ask one of them; I could count on my fingers the few occasions I managed simply to listen. And immediately afterwards, always, the shame: not at the behaviour, which was harmless enough, but at being unable to govern the impulse. I posted this and asked whether others knew the feeling. They did, and the thread ran long and serious into the afternoon, 90 comments in all.
