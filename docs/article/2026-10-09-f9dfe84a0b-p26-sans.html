At the same time, it was increasingly clear to the government that it would not just be difficult to remove the Jews – but positively dangerous to do so. Even the Jews’ most ardent detractors had to admit that having Jewish bankers in the city at a time of crisis was something of a boon. The diarist Marin Sanudo the Younger is a case in point. Although he complained bitterly about the ‘great number’ of Jews he saw in the street, he acknowledged that it was impossible to say anything because ‘they are needed’. And the more the war dragged on, the more essential they became.

The question facing Venice therefore shifted. Instead of focusing on how the Jews might be expelled, the government now began to ask itself how best it could take advantage of the Jews’ presence, while minimising the ‘risk’.
