小中大切換為無襯線

Where a land ethic blooms

All of us mammals have microbes in our guts, facilitating and regulating digestion. Large-bodied herbivores take this to an extreme – huge body cavities accommodate an intricate digestive system that shelters, feeds and harvests a community of microbes. Mouth, teeth, tongue, digestive tract and brain are all organised to harvest and process grass, leveraging the ability of microbes to consume the fibrous grasses and excrete easily digestible sugars.

In the tallgrass prairie, American bison select where to graze based, to some extent, on where they can maximise their intake of the plant resource to which they are best adapted. The ecological niche of an organism is perhaps most aptly translated as its palace, as the poet, translator and ecological activist Gary Snyder has re-discovered, and interpreted for us, in the work of the philosopher Dōgen. In the cycles of evolution, the efficiency and dominance of the tall grasses built a palace of greenery. The bison evolved to dwell in this palace. Among the tall grasses, they are fully at home.
