So from two completely different lines of inquiry you had one flavor of black holes being suspected from the X-ray, and you had another suspected predominantly by the radio.

LEVIN: So even though people started to theorize that these must be black holes, nobody had taken a picture of a black hole, not in the ’70s, ’80s, ’90s, the noughts. I think that was a surprise to people because we talked about them as being such compelling inhabitants of the universe, death states of stars, but we had actually technically never seen one directly. Never taken a portrait. And that’s really what you set out to do in many ways.

DOELEMAN: Yeah. I think the simplest way of saying it is that seeing is believing in a certain way. I mean, you can theorize something, but actually seeing it confers a reality on things that you don’t get otherwise. And there were some really important signatures, too. It wasn’t just seeing it. It was measuring it. Taking the measure of the thing not only gives you confidence in its reality, but you can test predictions.
