How to be a (Roman) man

In the late republic, attempts were made to codify guidance to aspiring orators. An anonymous treatise on rhetoric addressed To Herennius promises to help its reader present himself as a proper man through training in public speech. The author advises, for instance, against any sharp exclamation that ‘injures the voice and likewise jars the hearer, for it has about it something ignoble, suited rather to feminine outcry than to manly dignity in speaking.’ In On the Orator, Cicero has a character emphasise the importance of appropriate gestures in seeking to arouse particular emotions in one’s listeners: ‘all these emotions must be accompanied by gesture – … with this vigorous manly throwing out of the chest, borrowed not from the stage and the theatrical profession but from the parade ground or even from wrestling.’ The theatre had morally dubious associations; actors were often branded effeminate. Performer though he was, an orator would do well to cultivate a more military aura and disguise the degree to which his art drew on dramatic technique.
