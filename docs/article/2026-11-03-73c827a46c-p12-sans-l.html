小中大切換為襯線

The self-making world

Having begun his career in botany, in 1793 Lamarck was obliged to move, reluctantly, into zoology. This was in the midst of the Revolution, during the transitional moment when the main botanical garden in Paris – the King’s Garden – became the republican Garden of Plants under the auspices of a new National Museum of Natural History. The committee in charge of this transformation did not assign Lamarck to a chair in botany as he wanted, but instead to the lowliest chair in zoology: the Professorship of Insects and Worms. He would later tell his students that he’d been, at first, disappointed: if he couldn’t carry on studying botany, then why not charismatic megafauna, like lions? But then he’d had two hopeful thoughts. First, his assigned creatures constituted nine-tenths of the animal world, so he hadn’t been relegated to an unimportant corner of zoology. And, second, he imagined the simplest animals might reveal to him the foundation of animal life. Contemplating his insects and his worms with this faint gleam of hope, he created a new taxonomic category, ‘invertebrates’, and set to work studying them.
