The ghetto on the lagoon

When the Venetian Ghetto was established, of course, no one could have foreseen that it would have such a lasting legacy. Its founders never meant to lay down a marker for the future. It wasn’t the result of a long-term plan. In fact, it almost didn’t happen at all. It was a compromise that emerged out of a period of confusion, experimentation and failure – an attempt to balance the apparently irreconcilable demands of religious prejudice and economic need. It was a paradox, born of war and migration, fear and uncertainty. And, to understand how it came into being, we have to go right back to the arrival of the very first Jews in the Venetian lagoon.

By the time the Ghetto was founded, Jews were already a familiar presence in the Venetian lagoon. As early as the 10th century, Jewish merchants from the eastern Mediterranean are known to have travelled on board Venetian ships; and at least a few spent some time in the city itself. They were, however, a transitory presence, staying only for as long as it took to unload a cargo or conclude their business, and then moving on to harbours new.
