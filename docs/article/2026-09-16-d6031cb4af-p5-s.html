小中大切換為無襯線

Chasing la dolce vita

In my teens, I spent a month in Florence looking after the child of a couple of scientists, colleagues of my father. My role was to speak to the boy in English; his parents’ job was to improve my Italian. The moment I was alone with the seven-year-old, he locked himself in his room and refused to come out. As a consolation, one evening, I was entrusted to a 16-year-old nephew who took me to join his friends in a packed central piazza. Within minutes, the assembled youngsters were trying to identify my hybrid accent, crowding round with questions, trying out their English: ‘Are you French or British?’ ‘Which football team do you support?’ ‘Have you got a girlfriend?’ Revving their Vespas, blasting music I didn’t recognise, and wearing clothes I’d have avoided, they couldn’t make head nor tail of me. I escaped into Florence, only vaguely aware of where I was going. Leaning against a wall on the banks of the Arno, the Ponte Vecchio in the distance, I tried to convince myself that the surrounding ‘bellezza’ vaunted by my father was mine, that I fitted into the circus of Italian life I’d seen in Fellini films, but I felt I was a baffling anomaly, an impostor. The chameleon skills I’d always employed to fudge my way through existence had let me down. My hosts were in a state of panic on my return. Where had I been? I wasn’t going to be allowed out again. I’d failed as a childminder. And as an Italian.
