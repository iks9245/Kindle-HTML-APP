Barbara Ward’s vision

Poverty was such a significant issue for Ward to tackle because it created global political unrest and undermined local societies’ potential to prosper. How could it be overcome? She was persuaded that only a revolution could help humanity undermine global inequality. The postwar era was, for her, a particularly promising time for radical change: ‘I suppose we are all aware of the fact that we live in the most catastrophically revolutionary age that men have ever faced.’ In the early 1960s, she identified ‘ten or twenty’ revolutions still underway. She singled out two important calls for equality: postcolonial self-determination, which led to ‘the new peoples’ claim to national equality with the older states’, and the revolution of economic development, which aimed to reduce global inequality. Following her friend Walt Rostow’s theory of modernisation, she explained that some countries (typically the European imperial powers) were ‘sensationally richer’ but others had ‘not yet’ realised a revolutionary transformation despite their ‘desire to do so’. The challenge of her era was to figure out how to make this revolution come true.
